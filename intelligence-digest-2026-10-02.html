01

Money got dearer everywhere, and India took the hit from 3 sides at once

The big picture: high borrowing costs, a diesel shortage and foreign selling met in the same place.

In short
  • The cost of borrowing for the United States government touched its highest level since 2002.
  • Diesel became so scarce that the G7 countries agreed to empty part of their emergency stocks.
  • India's main index fell for an 8th week in a row, its longest losing run in 25 years.

The price of money touched a 24-year high

A bond yield is what a lender asks in return for lending money for a set time. The yield on 10-year US government bonds rose to 5.33%, the highest since 2002. Long-term borrowing costs in the UK and France also hit multi-year highs, as the pointers show.

  • United States — the 10-year yield rose to 5.33%; the Fed (America's central bank) had already raised its rate in September.
  • United Kingdom — long-term government yields hit 6% for the first time in almost 30 years.
  • Europe — France now pays 1.54% more than Germany on 10-year loans; its default insurance (CDS) is at a 13-year high.
  • Japan — the 10-year yield hit 3.126%, a 52-week high.
  • Then a turn — US jobs data came in very weak, yields fell for a few hours, then rose back to where they started.

So even bad news for the economy could not bring borrowing costs down for long. That tells you buyers of long bonds are scarce. The full story is in section 02.

Diesel ran short, and the rich countries opened their tanks

The war around Iran and the Strait of Hormuz (the narrow sea lane that carries much of the Gulf's oil) has cut the supply of refined fuels more than crude oil. The G7 group of rich countries agreed to release up to 100 million barrels of diesel and crude over 4 months, with diesel first.

The US had pushed Europe hard, threatening to stop selling American diesel abroad. China also stopped its fuel exports for October. So the world's 2 biggest swing suppliers of diesel both pulled back at the same time, which is why prices spiked. Section 03 has the details.

India's market fell for an 8th straight week

The Nifty 50, the index of India's biggest listed companies, closed at 22,421.95 on 1 October. That made 8 losing weeks in a row, a run seen only once before in a quarter of a century. Foreign investors sold heavily that day, as the map shows.

Why India is hurt more than its own economy deserves

One view is that the fall is mostly about outside forces: high US yields, a strong dollar and dear crude oil make foreign money leave all emerging markets. The opposite view is that India made it worse itself, with high taxes on share trading (STT, short-term and long-term capital gains tax) and too much red tape.

  • Outside forces — the US dollar index hit a 17-month high; the rupee is down about 6.5% this year, its worst year since 2022.
  • Home-made — several investors argued the government has been "unfriendly" to market investors through taxes; others said the real problem is over-regulation of business.
  • Domestic buyers — Indian funds (DIIs) bought about ₹10,042 crore on the same day foreigners sold ₹9,484 crore.

The 2 views lead to different fixes. If the cause is outside, the fall ends when US yields or crude fall. If the cause is home-made, it ends only when India changes tax or rules. Section 10 sets the 2 sides against each other.

AI is now the market almost everywhere

While India fell, US technology shares hit records, and Nvidia reached about $5.7 trillion in value. Companies tied to AI infrastructure now make up about 40% of the value of America's main share index, as section 09 shows.

So the world's money is crowding into one theme, and India has few listed AI winners. That is part of why foreign money flows to Taiwan, Korea and the US instead. Korea's exports jumped 83.5% in September, mostly on memory chips. Section 09 covers this.

02

The price of money: high yields, a weak jobs report and cracks in risky debt

Why borrowing stays dear even when the economy looks soft, and where the strain is showing first.

In short
  • US jobs growth almost stopped in September, but long-term borrowing costs barely fell.
  • The riskiest company bonds are being sold hard, and a record bond deal lost value within hours.
  • Europe's bond markets are showing stress not seen since the euro crisis.

A jobs report far below every guess

The US economy added only 29,000 jobs in September, against a guess of about 90,000. It was below the lowest estimate of any economist surveyed, and earlier months were revised down too.

There are 2 surveys, and they disagree. The company survey says hiring nearly stopped. The household survey says more people found work, but more people also started looking, so the unemployment rate still rose. So the labour market is cooling, not collapsing.

The Fed raised rates, and markets now doubt it will again

In September the Fed raised its main rate by a quarter point, calling it "removing accommodation". After the jobs report, betting markets cut the odds of another rise in October to about 17%, from near 75% only days earlier.

  • New rate — the Fed's target range is now 3.75%-4.00%, and chair Kevin Warsh hinted the Fed may skip October and move again in December.
  • The Fed's view — a Fed governor said the job market was "stabilising, even strengthening a bit", and the Fed may need "more time".
  • Inflation — the Fed's favourite price measure (PCE) rose 3.4% over 1 year in August; factory input prices hit 77.9 on the ISM survey.
  • The White House — President Trump said Warsh should have voted against the September rise; adviser Kevin Hassett said "we do not want to inflate our way out of debt".

So the Fed is caught between soft jobs and hard prices. One view is that the revised numbers prove the rise was a mistake made on bad data. The opposite view is that inflation from fuel and metals is still climbing, so cutting now would be the bigger mistake.

Long bonds: nobody wants to lend for 30 years

After the weak jobs data, the 10-year yield dropped to about 5.15%, then climbed back to 5.25%, erasing the whole move within hours. Goldman Sachs traders described the long end of the bond market as "still totally bidless", meaning buyers are missing.

One view is that bonds are now cheap and worth buying at more than 5%. The opposite view is that the US government still pays under 4% on its old debt, so as that debt is refinanced its interest bill will jump. Deficits would then grow toward $3 trillion, which means even more bonds to sell. Gold buyers use the same argument.

The Paramount bond that sank on its first day

Paramount Skydance sold about $52 billion of debt to pay for Warner Bros, including the largest junk bond ever. Banks said buyers had put in $109 billion of orders. Within hours the junk notes traded at about 96 cents on the dollar.

Investors complained to the banks about losses above $100 million. So the strong demand on paper was not real demand. When a record deal falls at once, lenders become more careful with the next one, and that raises costs for every weak borrower. The combined company will be called Skydance.

Private credit and AI debt

  • Blue Owl — investors asked to take out 16.8% of the shares of one of its private credit funds in a single quarter.
  • Most bet-against bonds — 9 of the 10 most shorted (bet-against) high-grade US bonds are long-dated bonds of Meta, Nvidia, Oracle, Alphabet and SpaceX; CoreWeave's bonds top the junk list.
  • KKR estimate — AI-linked debt is about $600 billion, or 6.3% of the US high-grade bond market, against a normal top-sector share of 2.6%.

So lenders are starting to worry about how the AI build-out is paid for. A KKR paper says AI could reach 20% of the high-grade index, with another $1.7 trillion of leases and purchase promises off the balance sheets.

Europe: a debt scare returns

France's budget trouble spread across Europe, and Italy's short-term borrowing costs over Germany's moved the most since 2020. France expects its interest bill to rise 15% next year to €91 billion, almost double its core defence budget.

At the same time, euro-area inflation rose to 3.8% in September, a 3-year high, mostly from energy. So the European Central Bank cannot easily buy bonds to calm markets without making inflation worse. A London exchange declared "stressed market conditions" in bond futures for a day.

American homes and households

The average 30-year US mortgage rate rose to 7.60%, a 3-year high, and reports said house showings "have stopped". A US plan to let lenders use fewer credit bureaus knocked Fair Isaac shares sharply lower.

The link back to India is direct. High US yields keep the dollar strong, and a strong dollar is the main reason foreign funds keep selling Indian shares, as section 04 shows.

03

Oil and diesel: crude is flowing again, but the fuel trucks run on is not

Why a barrel near $100 still hides a diesel crisis, and how it reaches Indian petrol pumps.

In short
  • Crude oil from the Gulf has recovered to about 3/4 of pre-war levels, but refined fuels have not.
  • The G7 agreed to release up to 100 million barrels, with diesel first, after US pressure.
  • In India, private pumps limited diesel sales because factories rushed to buy cheap retail diesel.

The G7 release: what was agreed

France's President Macron chaired a G7 video call and announced the deal. The International Energy Agency (IEA, the rich countries' energy club) will coordinate it. Diesel comes first, in the first 20 days.

  • No export bans — G7 members promised not to block energy exports to each other; rumours of bans had pushed prices up.
  • Refinery timing — countries will spread out refinery repairs so plants do not shut at the same time.
  • Old barrels — some IEA countries did not fully deliver a March release; the open question is how many new barrels actually reach the market.

A ban that was really a lever

The US had told Germany and France to release diesel stocks or face a US ban on diesel exports. US Treasury and energy officials pushed in public too. President Trump then admitted a US export ban could raise American petrol prices.

One view is that the ban threat was a bluff used to force Europe to act, and it worked. The opposite view is that Europe gave up reserves it needs for winter. France had backed a release early; Brussels and Berlin resisted. So the US won the fight, but Europe is now thinner on emergency stocks before the cold months.

Why Europe has the bigger diesel problem

Diesel and similar fuels are about 41% of Europe's oil use, against about 19% in the US. Europe's trucks, farms and many cars run on diesel. So the same shortage hurts Europe about twice as much.

China stopped selling fuel abroad

Chinese refiners suspended fuel exports for October, and PetroChina cancelled some cargoes. China usually ships diesel and petrol to Singapore, Malaysia, Australia, Vietnam, Bangladesh and the Philippines. Asian diesel prices rose to 2-week highs on the news.

One view is that China is nervous: it has been drawing down its own oil stocks, so supply may be less secure than tanker data suggest. Another view says the US may use its sway over Gulf oil to press China on rare earth minerals (metals needed for magnets and chips). China buys about 25% of its oil from the Gulf. If it cuts refinery runs, the world loses even more diesel.

The Gulf is pumping again, slowly

  • Saudi Arabia — loaded 11 tankers at once from 4 terminals; its East-West pipeline runs above 80% of capacity, sending more oil to the Red Sea.
  • Overall — Macron said Hormuz plus the Red Sea route now carry a little more than 3/4 of pre-war volumes.
  • Attacks go on — Iran has launched about 30 drone and 10 missile attacks a week on shipping since early August; at least 13 ships were hit since 10 September.
  • A supertanker — a tanker was set ablaze in Hormuz as the US sent 10,000 more troops and a 3rd aircraft carrier to the region.

So crude is coming back, but every ship still runs a risk. Insurers and shipowners charge for that risk, which keeps fuel dear even when the barrel price eases.

India: pumps that rationed diesel

In India, prices at state-run pumps are held steady, so retail diesel became cheaper than diesel sold in bulk to industry. Factories, telecom firms, hotels and hospitals began filling up at petrol pumps. Jio-bp limited buyers to 50 litres per visit and Nayara Energy to 70-200 litres.

  • Government — the oil ministry said "nobody is allowed to put a cap on sales" and asked both to lift the limits.
  • Size — India has about 100,000 pumps; state-run oil companies own about 90%; Nayara runs more than 7,000.
  • Exports to Russia — a research group said Russia imported a record 172,000 tonnes of fuel in August, mostly from India; about 70% came from Nayara's Vadinar refinery.
  • Demand — diesel sales rose 4.85% and petrol 7% from April to September.

So the shortage shows up in India as a price gap, not empty tanks. State oil companies take the loss by selling below cost, while private pumps try to avoid it. That loss lands on the government's books later.

Shadow tankers hide under Russia's flag

A research group, CREA, found that 107 ships joined Russia's ship registry in 18 months, and 93 of them were already under Western sanctions. Ships with no proper flag can be boarded by foreign navies, so they took Russia's flag for legal cover.

The catch is that a Russian flag bans them from Western ports. So the world's oil fleet is splitting into 2 groups, one serving the West and one serving Russia and its buyers. That raises shipping costs for everyone, and it ties back to the US sanctions law in section 09.

The wider bill

A UN study said governments may spend more than $1 trillion on fuel subsidies this year. World food prices also rose in September, and crop prices had their biggest quarterly jump since the Ukraine invasion. India's external affairs minister warned of a "major food crisis" in the coming months.

So the fuel shock is turning into a food shock, which keeps inflation sticky and central banks wary, closing the loop with section 02. India is building its own escape routes: an LNG-powered train now runs between Sabarmati and Mahesana, cutting diesel use by 40%, and a ₹23,731 crore biogas scheme is covered in section 05.

04

Inside India's market: 8 weeks down, foreigners selling, and an IPO boom anyway

Where the selling hit, how cheap shares now are, and why new listings still drew crowds.

In short
  • Foreign investors sold Indian shares on almost every recent trading day, and local funds bought almost all of it.
  • The Nifty 50 is now cheaper than its 5-year average, but small companies are still dear.
  • September was the biggest month for new listings this year, even as the market fell.

The longest losing run in 25 years

The Nifty fell 8 weeks in a row. The only longer run in a quarter century was 9 weeks in 2001, after a stock-market scam and the dot-com crash. A Reuters chart blamed "higher crude, bond yield surge".

05

India's economy: strong tax and factory numbers, but power, rain and rules are the weak spots

What the September data says, where the economy is under strain, and the policies announced to fix it.

In short
  • Tax collections, factory orders and digital payments all grew strongly in September.
  • India's power shortage rose 5-fold, the worst September since 2017, because of weak rain and low coal stocks.
  • The government approved big plans for power lines, batteries and biogas to fix the gaps.

The September report card

Most monthly data for India looked healthy. The finance ministry estimated growth of 7.3% for the July-September quarter, well above the RBI's 6.4% forecast. The first quarter grew 7.8%.
